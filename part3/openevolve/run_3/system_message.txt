Levers you control inside the EVOLVE-BLOCK
------------------------------------------
Inside `jobs:` (per job):
- `node`: "node-a-8core" or "node-b-4core".
- `cpuset`: Linux cpu-list string passed to `taskset -c`, e.g. "0-3" or "2,3". Empty string = no pinning.
- `threads`: integer for PARSEC's `-n` argument.
- `cpu_request` / `cpu_limit`: Kubernetes resource strings, e.g. "400m", "2", "6". Requests must fit on the chosen node (after memcached's 1500m on node-b). If they don't fit, the Pod stays Pending and the evaluator times out = infeasible.
- `memory_request` / `memory_limit`: strings like "512Mi", "4Gi". Respect node-b's ~4 GiB ceiling.

Inside `schedule:` (list of action strings, executed top-to-bottom):
- `submit <job-name>`: kubectl apply; returns immediately. The job runs when Kubernetes places it.
- `sleep <seconds>`: wall-clock delay (float allowed).

Every job listed in `jobs:` must be submitted exactly once. After the schedule is exhausted the evaluator joins every submitted job — you do not need (and cannot issue) an explicit wait. Ordering between two jobs is expressed by either (a) a `sleep` between their submits, or (b) giving them `cpu_request`s that together exceed the chosen node's capacity so Kubernetes queues the second one until the first releases resources.

Hard rules (breaking any = infeasibility score)
-----------------------------------------------
- Do not change a job's `image` value.
- Do not change the top-level keys `jobs` / `schedule`, do not rename a job.
- Do not put `parsec-radix` on node-b-4core.
- Do not submit a job twice or forget a job.
- Do not add new DSL verbs beyond `submit` / `sleep`.

Feedback you receive after each iteration
-----------------------------------------
The evaluator returns the following artifacts along with the numeric score; use them to inform the next mutation:

- `min_p95_us`, `mean_p95_us`, `max_p95_us`: memcached p95 latency in microseconds across the measurement rows taken while your batch jobs ran. SLO is 1000 μs.
- `slo_headroom_us`: `1000 - max_p95_us`. Positive = headroom; zero/negative = you already violated. A large positive headroom (e.g. > 400 μs) means you left speed on the table — push harder: co-locate more on node-b, raise thread counts, or overlap submissions. Small headroom (< 100 μs) means back off.
- `per_pod_summary`: a table in the form
        job                     node                wait (s)   run (s)   total (s)   Δrun (s)
        ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
        parsec-blackscholes     node-b-4core-xkbd        2.0     141.0       143.0      +12.4
        ...
        ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
        MAKESPAN (wall)                                                        188.0
        p95 min/avg/max (us) : 304 / 417 / 491
  `wait` = Pending + image-pull time (long wait = resource request too big or node full); `run` = actual execution; `total` = wait + run. `Δrun (s)` is signed vs the previous iteration (`+` = this job got slower, `-` = faster) — use it to attribute regressions to specific pods rather than guessing. The final `MAKESPAN (wall)` row is what the score is based on.
- `ooms`: pods killed by the Linux OOM killer. Non-empty = that pod's `memory_limit`, or the memory pressure on its node, was too tight.

Exploration
-----------
The seed scheduler.yaml is one valid point in the search space; it is not a target. Your previous runs plateaued with large SLO headroom (>400 μs max_p95 under 600 μs): that means there is speed to be won. Radically reordering the schedule, moving jobs between nodes, changing thread counts, merging `wait` barriers, inserting or removing `sleep`s, and packing more concurrent submissions on a node are all fair game whenever `slo_headroom_us` is large. Candidate structural moves include: starting two heavy node-a jobs overlapped instead of sequentially, co-locating a second small job on node-b alongside whatever is already there, giving the node-a stragglers more threads, or moving a job that currently waits a long time to the other node. Do not stay close to the seed just because it works — look for a structural win and trade part of the headroom for speed.
